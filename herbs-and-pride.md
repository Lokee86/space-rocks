# Herbs and Pride

The dung was the clue that broke it open.

Sorina Hăulică had spent nine mornings hiking the ridgeline in the thin Carpathian mist, searching for divots in the frost, bent bracken, the shiny smear of dragon saliva on limestone. Steluța, all forty-two feet of her, was too old and too clever to leave a trail a Ministry trainee could follow. She'd been doing this for sixty-three years of her ninety-seven—longer than Sorina had been alive, longer than the reserve had even existed—and she knew the silent paths through the saddleback pass better than any creature on the mountain.

But she had not accounted for her own digestion.

Sorina knelt. The dung was the usual deep teal of a Romanian Longhorn's droppings, but studded through it like garnets in clay were hundreds of tiny red seed husks. Not juniper. Not rowan. She fished one out, rolled it between her glove, and her stomach sank.

*Vinca petrocarpae.* Stone-periwinkle. Only grew in one place within fifty miles: the limestone scree slopes above Văleni village, three valleys east.

The same Văleni whose sheep pens had been mysteriously battered open four nights running. The same Văleni whose mayor had sent a howling owl to the Ministry of Magic two weeks ago.

"Ah, Doamna Steluța," Sorina muttered, straightening with a wince—her knees were not what they'd been at thirty-five. "What are you *feeding* yourself?"

The Ministry clerk arrived on schedule, which meant he arrived exactly when the Portkey deposited him, which was 9:47 AM on a Tuesday.

He carried a leather satchel, a collapsible umbrella, and the energetic resignation of a civil servant who had long ago accepted that his job description contained the phrase *liaise with dragon reserves* without any corresponding hazard pay.

"I'm here about the sheep attacks," he said, showing Sorina his identification—a brass badge that glowed faintly green. "I have a Form 7-A, a Form 12-C(b), three copies of the incident log from Văleni's village council, and—" he patted his satchel "—a preliminary liability assessment. I'd like to get these signed and filed before lunch, if that's acceptable."

"The dragon isn't attacking sheep."

"Four nights," said Mr. Pumble, peering at a memo. "Pens breached. Sheep scattered. No sheep actually *eaten*, which is peculiar, but considerable property damage. Mayor Cojocaru is quite cross."

"She wandered through the pens. She wasn't hunting."

"Is there a difference, from the mayor's perspective?"

Sorina didn't answer. She led him to the aerie office, a stone cottage built into the eastern cliff face, and set a pot of tea on the woodstove while he arranged his papers on her worktable with the fastidiousness of a man assembling a bomb.

He was not wrong to be efficient. The paperwork was the least of it. What mattered was what she had found at dawn.

She had followed the seed husks back along Steluța's likely path: south along the ridgeline, down through the pine brake at Gura Râului, then a hard scramble over the limestone ridge into the Văleni valley. And there, on the south-facing scree slope above the village, she had found the whole answer.

A thick patch of *Vinca petrocarpae*—stone-periwinkle, the old books called it—spread across a sunny depression in the rockfall. More than half of it had been cropped off at the stem. Beside the patch, pressed into the damp soil, a dragon-sized bed where Steluța had lain, contentedly chewing her cud, watching the lights of the village below.

Sorina had brought her rheumatism salve to every aerie inspection for the past seven years. Steluța's arthritis was no secret—she'd been stiffening up each winter, slower on the wing, grumpier in the mornings. The dragon hadn't told her, of course. Dragons didn't. But Sorina had noticed. She'd adjusted the perches. Added a heated stone in the old girl's cave. Brewed willow-bark tinctures and mixed them into her feed.

But she'd missed the real problem.

*Vinca petrocarpae* was a potent anti-inflammatory, but it didn't grow within the reserve's boundaries. It required thin soil, high drainage, and the particular mineral composition of that limestone scree. Steluța had found it on one of her flights, tried it, and felt better than she had in years. Better than Sorina's willow bark had ever made her feel.

And now the dragon couldn't stop.

---

Dr. Ileana Florescu's cottage sat at the edge of Văleni, exactly where the village's cobbled lane frayed into mud track and then into mountain. Her garden was a controlled riot of medicinal herbs behind a low stone wall. Onions hung drying under the eaves. A disgruntled chicken regarded Sorina from atop a beehive.

Sorina had not spoken to Ileana in eleven years.

The feud had started over a mooncalf, or possibly over a manure dispute, or possibly over something so trivial that neither woman could now recall its original shape. What remained was a durable, comfortable hostility—the kind two stubborn people maintain because admitting it was pointless would also mean admitting they'd been childish for a decade.

Sorina knocked.

Ileana opened the door. She was the same age as Sorina, give or take a year, with grey-streaked black hair tied back in a practical knot and the no-nonsense expression of someone who had, at some point in her life, delivered a breech calf with nothing but a length of twine and a strong stomach.

"I need your help," said Sorina.

Ileana stared at her for a long moment. Then she leaned against the doorframe, crossed her arms, and produced a smile that was not entirely unkind.

"Say that again."

"Don't make me."

"Say it, or I'm closing this door and you can go wrestle your dragon problem with your bare hands."

Sorina's jaw tightened. The wind carried the distant sound of sheep bells from the upper meadow. She thought about Steluța, stiff and aching, making that long flight every night because her keeper hadn't been clever enough to solve the problem on her own.

"I need your help," Sorina repeated, and this time it came out quieter, and truer.

Ileana's smile faded into something more thoughtful. She pushed the door open wider.

"Come in, then. I'll put the kettle on."

---

Inside, the cottage smelled of dried lavender and something sharper—wormwood, maybe, or silver leaf. The kitchen was small but immaculate, copper pots arranged by size, a mortar and pestle still dusty with ground herbs.

"The dragon," said Ileana, settling into a chair. "Steluța, yes? The old Longhorn."

"How did you know it was her?"

"The village has guessed. She flies low over the pass. People have seen her silhouette against the moon. Mayor Cojocaru knows it's a reserve dragon, but he won't say so publicly because then he'd have to admit he's known for weeks and only reported it now because the damage got bad enough that his cousin's sheep were involved." She sipped her tea. "What's she after?"

"*Vinca petrocarpae.*"

Ileana's eyebrows rose. "Stone-periwinkle. That's a strong anti-inflammatory. Strong enough that a dragon with chronic joint pain—" She stopped. "Ah. She's old. She hurts."

"Yes."

"Have you been treating her?"

"Willow bark. Heat stones. I adjusted her perches. I didn't—" Sorina set her cup down. "I didn't think to check what she was finding on her own. I assumed I had it handled."

Ileana was quiet for a moment. "You were trying. That's more than most keepers would do for a dragon past breeding age. Most would just let the Ministry pension her off to a sanctuary and forget about her."

"I'm not 'most keepers.'"

"No. You're not." Ileana set her cup down. "The stone-periwinkle only grows on the Văleni scree. The soil chemistry is specific. You can't just dig it up and transplant it to the reserve—it'd die in a season."

"Then we need another solution."

"Three options," said Ileana, ticking them off on her fingers. "One: you negotiate trade access with the village. Let the dragon forage legally. Mayor Cojocaru will ask for compensation, and he will be unreasonable about it, and you will have to fight him through the Ministry bureaucracy for months. Two: I can prepare a warding charm for the sheep pens. It won't stop a determined dragon if she really wants in, but it will make the pens *feel* wrong to her—uncomfortable, like the ground is shifting. It would break the habit."

"And three?"

"Three: we propagate the stone-periwinkle ourselves. I have a greenhouse. I can adjust the soil, control the drainage. It would take a few seasons to establish a supply, but I could grow enough to keep the dragon supplied without her having to cross the valley."

Sorina stared at her. "You'd do that?"

"On three conditions." Ileana set down her cup and fixed Sorina with a look that was disarmingly direct. "One: you stop using that sulfurous fertilizer the reserve imports from Bulgaria. It's ruining the soil in the lower pastures and it's giving your young dragons indigestion. I've seen the dung. It's not right."

"It's cheaper than the organic blend."

"I know. That's why I'm saying it. Two: when I say 'a few seasons,' I mean you need to commit to feeding Steluța a transitional anti-inflammatory in the meantime. I can brew a tincture of meadowsweet and white willow bark, fortified with a Preservation Charm, that will manage her pain until the periwinkle supply is stable. You'll need to administer it daily. Can you do that?"

"Yes. Easily."

"And three." Ileana paused. She picked an invisible thread from her sleeve. "You come to dinner. Next Saturday. I make a decent *mici*, and the market had good peppers this week. We can call it even."

The silence stretched. Outside, a crow called from the beech tree.

Sorina thought about eleven years of narrow-eyed greetings at the market. Eleven years of taking the long way around the village square to avoid passing Ileana's stall. Eleven years of petty pride, and for what? A disagreement neither of them could even remember.

"I don't know if I can promise to stop using the Bulgarian fertilizer," she said. "The budget—"

"Negotiable."

"—but I can promise to try."

"That's all I'm asking."

"And I'll be here Saturday. What time?"

"Seven. Bring wine."

---

Mr. Pumble finished his paperwork at 2:13 PM, signed in four places, stamped in two, and accepted a cup of tea with the quiet satisfaction of a man who would soon be home.

"The mayor will want official confirmation that the situation is resolved," he said, tucking the forms into his satchel. "I'll file a preliminary action report. Proposed resolution: herbal foraging rights negotiation, pending village council approval, with temporary deterrent charms on the sheep pens. That should satisfy the Ministry's requirement for documented escalation."

"Will it?"

"Frankly?" He smiled, a little tired, a little genuine. "The Ministry doesn't actually care about the sheep. They care about the paperwork. If there's a file that says 'ongoing negotiation, no casualties, low risk profile,' everyone signs off and moves on to the next thing. No fines. No escalations. Just a footnote in the quarterly report."

"And the charms on the pens?"

"I'll make a note that they were applied by an accredited local practitioner—Dr. Florescu's credentials are in order, I checked—and that the reserve keeper facilitated. It's all very tidy."

For a moment Sorina thought about telling him the whole story. About Steluța's aching joints and the long flights in the dark and the herb that grew only where she couldn't reach it. About the woman she'd been feuding with for eleven years and the dinner invitation she'd just accepted.

But some things didn't fit on Form 7-A.

"Thank you, Mr. Pumble."

"Thank *you*, Mrs. Hăulică. For filing everything before lunch."

He touched his satchel, offered a small nod, and stepped through the Portkey's shimmer with a soft pop. The air sealed behind him.

---

That evening, Sorina hiked up to the high aerie where Steluța lay curled on her heated stone, steam rising gently from her scales. The dragon's eyes cracked open—one amber slit, ancient and knowing—and she lifted her great head just enough to sniff the air.

"No more sneaking out," Sorina said, sitting down on the moss beside her. "I'm fixing it. Properly. Just give me a little time."

Steluța exhaled a plume of warm, herb-scented breath. She lowered her head to her foreclaws and closed her eyes again.

Somewhere in the valley below, a sheep bleated. The wind carried the sound up the slope, thin and distant, and Steluța's ear flicked.

"Don't even think about it."

The dragon made a sound like grinding stones—the Longhorn equivalent of a chuckle—and settled deeper into her sleep.

Sorina stayed until the stars came out. They were bright above the Carpathians, cold and clear, the same stars Steluța had flown under on her nightly raids. The same stars Ileana would see from her garden while she watered her stone-periwinkle cuttings.

Saturday at seven. Sorina had already picked a bottle from the reserve's cellar.

It was, she decided, a start.
